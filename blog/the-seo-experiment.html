The SEO experiment I'm running

Instead of vague “I should blog more” guilt, I’m treating this site like a small, public SEO lab with clear rules and a scoreboard.

A small blog with big intentions: one URL, one experiment at a time.

For years I’ve given other people SEO advice that I only half-followed for myself. Classic cobbler-with-no-shoes situation. Outwit is my attempt to fix that — not with a massive content calendar, but with a small, opinionated experiment I can actually stick with.

The idea is simple: treat this blog like a tiny search engine project, write about the things I’m genuinely doing, and see what happens when you stack modest, consistent effort over 12–18 months.

The rules of the experiment

I don’t want to accidentally turn this into a job, so I’m putting guardrails around it. Roughly:

  • One meaningful post per month, minimum. No filler. If it doesn’t feel like a real thought, it doesn’t ship.
  • Each post gets a clear search intent and a handful of phrases I’d like it to rank for, chosen before I write.
  • Edits are allowed, rewrites are allowed, but no deleting posts just because they didn’t perform.

The point isn’t to “hack” SEO — it’s to see what happens when you actually respect it.

On the technical side, I’m keeping things as boring as possible: static HTML, fast hosting, clean internal links, and structured data that tells Google exactly what each page is.

What I’m measuring (and ignoring)

I care about a short list of metrics: impressions, clicks, average position on a handful of queries, and how many real conversations, emails, or projects those visitors turn into. That’s it.

What I’m ignoring: vanity social numbers, generic “time on page” screenshots, or any metric that makes me feel productive without changing what I actually do next.

What might break (and why I’m okay with that)

Experiments only work if you’re willing to be wrong in public. It’s possible the posts I enjoy writing are not the posts that move the graph. It’s possible search volatility or some algorithm change makes the data noisy. That’s fine.

The worst case scenario is that I end up with a year’s worth of essays about work I was already doing. The best case is that the compounding nature of boring SEO makes this tiny corner of the internet a surprisingly valuable asset.
