Building assets instead of agencies

Client work pays well and drains fast. Assets are slower, quieter, and much harder to walk away from once they start compounding.

A whiteboard full of client projects, and a small column on the side labeled “assets.” The column is what I care about now.

I’ve spent most of my career selling time packaged as expertise. Strategy decks, campaigns, websites, retainers. It’s good work with good people, but structurally it all has the same flaw: when you stop showing up, the graph stops moving.

Nothing is wrong with that model. Agencies are just honest about the trade. You rent your brain and your team to other people’s problems. But somewhere along the way I realized I wanted more of my energy going into things I owned — assets that could keep working when the calendar was blank.

What I mean by “assets”

“Asset” is an overloaded word, so here’s my working definition: something you can build once that continues to create value with less and less incremental effort. That can be software, content, a small media property, even a local lead-gen site that throws off a handful of calls a week.

The common pattern is leverage. You trade upfront complexity for downstream simplicity. Instead of selling ten new projects next quarter, you can improve one thing that already exists and watch the delta ripple out.

Agencies monetize motion. Assets monetize stillness — the part where the thing just sits there and quietly works.

Why this matters for how I work

Once you see the difference, it’s hard to unsee. A proposal-heavy week now looks like a necessary means to an end, not the whole game. When I think about new opportunities, I ask a different set of questions:

  • Does this create an asset, or only a paycheck?
  • Will I care about the thing that exists at the end of this project a year from now?
  • If this went unexpectedly well, what would it turn into — more clients or more ownership?

Small shifts, not a dramatic exit

I’m not burning the boats on client work. I like helping good businesses grow. The shift is quieter than that: a few hours a week reserved for building my own small internet properties, writing here, and treating them with the same seriousness I’d give a paying client.

In a decade, I don’t want a portfolio that’s just a list of people I helped. I want a messy little family of assets — sites, products, experiments — that tell the story of what I chose to build when nobody was asking.
