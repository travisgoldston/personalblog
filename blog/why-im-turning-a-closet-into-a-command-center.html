Why I'm turning a closet into a command center

Most people want a bigger office. I wanted a smaller one. Here's why constraints make better spaces — and better decisions.

The closet before demo. 5'6" wide, 2'4" deep, and full of possibility.

There's a closet in our spare bedroom that hasn't held clothes in months. It's been holding potential. Five and a half feet wide, just under two and a half deep. Most people walk past a space like that. I kept walking back to it.

The idea was simple: take the smallest room in the house and make it the most intentional. Not a whole room converted into an office — a closet. Doors off. Three walls. A U-shaped desk built from IKEA SÄLJAN countertops, a pair of BESTÅ media cabinets underneath, and enough vertical storage to keep everything within arm's reach.

Constraints as a design language

The thing nobody tells you about small spaces is that they force clarity. When you have 400 square feet, you can be vague about what goes where. When you have 13, every square inch is a decision. There's no room — literally — for "I'll figure it out later."

That's what drew me to the project. Not the aesthetics (though I have opinions about those too). The discipline. A closet office is a forcing function for figuring out what actually matters in your workday.

The best workspaces aren't the biggest ones. They're the ones where nothing is accidental.

The engineering nobody sees

Here's where it gets real. A floating desk sounds simple until you're staring at three walls, two corners, and a countertop that needs to span 66 inches without sagging. SÄLJAN is particleboard with a laminate wrap — not exactly structural lumber. Without proper support, you're looking at a bow in the center within six months.

The solution: ledger boards lag-bolted to the studs on all three walls, heavy-duty L-brackets every 16 inches on center, and a thin plywood anti-sag spine bonded to the underside of the countertop. The brackets do the heavy lifting. The spine prevents flex. The ledger board distributes load across the full length instead of point-loading at each bracket.

What I got wrong the first time

I originally planned for brackets every 24 inches. Ran the numbers, talked to my dad — a master electrician who's been mounting heavy things to walls for 30 years — and he didn't even hesitate: 16" OC, no exceptions. The extra brackets cost about $12 total. The peace of mind is worth more.

Why this matters beyond the build

I keep coming back to the idea that how you build a workspace reflects how you think about work itself. A big desk with nothing on it is a vibe. A tight desk where everything has a reason is a system.

This closet is going to hold three monitors, a laptop, a mic, a camera, and probably a dog crate built into the cabinet run (yes, really). It's going to be the space where I run Boldspark, write this blog, and think through the next ten years. All from a closet that used to hold winter coats.

Sometimes the best move isn't to go bigger. It's to go smaller on purpose.
